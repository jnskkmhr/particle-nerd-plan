Inputs: MPM particle motion, mass, gravity, and rigid-box geometry. MPM reaction forces are withheld until evaluation.

Upward is positive. Each contact force appears with opposite signs on the two objects it connects.

This schematic extends the following three-particle example to include both rigid objects. The box can move vertically; the ground slab is fixed by an external support. B is the magnitude of the upward force of particle 1 on the box and the downward force of the box on particle 1. S12 is upward on particle 1 and downward on particle 2. S23 is upward on particle 2 and downward on particle 3. G is upward on particle 3 and downward on the ground. U is the signed net external actuator or holding force on the box; R is the signed external force holding the ground slab fixed. Weights are positive magnitudes acting downward. There are no other forces in this example. Summing the five equations cancels B, S12, S23, and G. This removes internal forces from the total balance but also removes the contact forces we want to reconstruct. For specified accelerations and known weights, the six unknowns are B, S12, S23, G, U, R. The five independent balances admit the variation: increase B, S12, S23, G, and R by the same amount, and decrease U by that amount. Every acceleration remains unchanged. This corresponds to the next slide's stationary example with a stronger downward holding force on the box and a stronger upward ground support. If U is independently known and the box acceleration is measured, the box equation instead determines its net contact force in this simple chain. In a forward coupled simulation the next box acceleration is itself an output, so one cannot assume it is independently known to close reconstruction. This is an exact one-dimensional counterexample, not simulation data. The three particles each weigh 10 N and have zero acceleration. Let B be the downward box force, S12 and S23 be compressive internal forces, and G be the upward ground force. The individual balances are S12-B-10=0, S23-S12-10=0, G-S23-10=0. Thus S12=B+10, S23=B+20, G=B+30. There are three independent scalar equations for four unknown force magnitudes. Any nonnegative B gives a compressive solution. Adding 100 N to all four unknowns changes the box and ground reactions while leaving every particle equation unchanged. The change (100,100,100,100) is a null-space direction of the balance matrix and affects the body reactions. Known contact locations and internal action-reaction are already included. This is stronger than showing ambiguity only in the summed system. It does not prove that every three-dimensional particle scene has ambiguous body reactions; that must be checked for the actual geometry and constraints. It also does not claim that both states satisfy a specified elastic or granular constitutive law with identical history. Such a law is exactly the additional information omitted by force balance alone. The fixed box has an unmeasured holding reaction, so its own equilibrium does not provide a known applied load. This describes the existing nonlearned force reconstruction, not a trained particle motion model. It solves three translational force-balance equations per material particle. Particle pairs within the neighbor radius carry one nonnegative central-force coefficient. A candidate particle–body contact carries four nonnegative friction-ray coefficients. The positive quadratic regularization penalizes these coefficients; it is not exactly a penalty on the norm of each resultant contact force, because that depends on its ray representation. Appending sqrt(regularization) times the identity with a zero target is equivalent to soft equations favoring zero coefficients. It makes the objective strictly convex in exact arithmetic but does not establish physical correctness. The evaluated baseline solves reached their iteration limit. The code does not read the recorded particle radii, rigid-body velocities or accelerations, Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, internal friction, deformation state, or material history. It computes body torque from contact-force moment arms but does not solve rigid-body dynamics or particle angular-momentum equations. The 4 cm contact shell is a geometric heuristic, not measured grain overlap. The separate DEM-inspired implementation and its measurements are described on the following slides.

k: contact stiffness; c: damping coefficient; μ: contact friction. Ground and moving bodies use the same law with their own geometry and surface velocity.
Source: LAMMPS granular contact laws and particle–wall contacts. The equations show a linear spring–damper model; Hertz–Mindlin is an alternative.
DEM means discrete element method. The equations illustrate one noncohesive linear spring–dashpot model, not all DEM formulations. For positive overlap, the normal force is the positive part of stiffness times overlap plus damping times signed closing speed. Use zero force after separation; do not apply a damping force across a gap. Tangential velocity is measured at the contact point relative to the other particle or rigid surface, including rotational contributions where represented. Transport tangential history into the changing contact plane, update it over the contact interval, correct it when the Coulomb bound is reached, and reset it when contact is lost. Static friction may remain nonzero at zero instantaneous tangential velocity because of stored tangential displacement. The force on the other object is equal and opposite. Particle–particle contacts can use the same law structure with appropriate radii and contact parameters. Hertz–Mindlin models provide nonlinear normal and tangential relationships involving elastic parameters and contact geometry. These are physical model parameters, so nonlearned does not mean parameter-free. 28 / CONTACT-FORCE RECONSTRUCTION IMPLEMENTED APPROXIMATION

From particle motion to force on the rigid box

Inputs and outputs of one force-estimation step

INPUT · particles

Current positions xᵢⁿ, masses mᵢ, and predicted next velocities v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹.

INPUT · rigid box

Current center cⁿ, orientation Rⁿ, linear velocity Vⁿ, angular velocity ωⁿ; mass m, inertia I, and box size.

OUTPUT

Material-on-box force F and torque τ; contact-corrected box velocities V⁺, ω⁺ and per-particle impulse −jᵢ.

Also supplied: Δt, gravity g, friction μ, contact offset δ, effective mass scale κ, penetration coefficient β; previous impulses if history is enabled.

This routine estimates contact loads. Particle positions and box pose are held fixed inside it. Newton advances the rigid-body pose separately.

1. Compute the box velocity without contact

Keep the current box pose fixed. Integrate gravity and the rigid body’s gyroscopic term for one timestep.

V* = Vⁿ + Δt g
ω* = ωⁿ − Δt I⁻¹ [ωⁿ × (I ωⁿ)]

I = Rⁿ Ilocal (Rⁿ)ᵀ is inertia about the box center of mass, expressed in world coordinates. * denotes the temporary velocity before contact.

Output: V*, ω*. No particle integration or position update occurs here. For zero initial spin, ω* = 0. Gravity is the only applied load in this example.

Example: Δt = 0.005 s and Vⁿ = 0 give V*z = −0.04905 m/s before contact.

2. Evaluate material velocity at the box surface

For particle i, find its nearest point sᵢ on the current box surface. Fit a local velocity gradient Gᵢ from neighboring particle velocities.

ûᵢ = v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ + Gᵢ (sᵢ − xᵢⁿ)
rᵢ = sᵢ − cⁿ   ·   dᵢ = signed distance − δ

Gᵢ is a regularized local affine fit. dᵢ > 0 means separation; dᵢ < 0 means penetration relative to the offset box surface. δ = 0 in the current selected model.

Output per contact: material velocity ûᵢ, lever arm rᵢ, gap dᵢ, and normal nᵢ pointing from material toward the box. Keep particles already touching or able to reach the box during Δt.

The force routine reads v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹; it does not produce it. Here it comes from recordings; eventually NeRD must supply it.

3. Iterate contact impulses and box velocity

Start at V*, ω*, jᵢ = 0. Iterate contacts: they can push the box, never pull.

uᵢ = ûᵢ − (jᵢ − α jᵢprev) / mᵢeff   ,   mᵢeff = κ mᵢ
wᵢ = nᵢ · (V + ω × rᵢ − uᵢ) + bᵢ
kᵢ = 1/m + (rᵢ × nᵢ)ᵀ I⁻¹ (rᵢ × nᵢ) + 1/mᵢeff
jᵢn ← max(0, jᵢn − wᵢ / kᵢ)
‖jᵢt‖ ≤ μ jᵢn   ·   friction opposes relative sliding

jᵢ is impulse on the box; n and t denote normal and tangential components. bᵢ = [max(dᵢ, 0) + β min(dᵢ, 0)] / Δt allows approach across a gap and corrects penetration.

For each impulse change Δjᵢ: V ← V + Δjᵢ / m and ω ← ω + I⁻¹(rᵢ × Δjᵢ). Stop when impulse changes are small. Output: V⁺, ω⁺ and jᵢ.

α weights the transported previous impulse (0 = no history). uᵢ is an auxiliary velocity; stored particle velocities stay fixed.

4. Convert the momentum correction into force

Sum the contact impulses on the box. Divide by Δt to obtain the force and torque over this interval.

Σᵢ jᵢ = m (V⁺ − V*)   ⇒   F = Σᵢ jᵢ / Δt
Σᵢ rᵢ × jᵢ = I (ω⁺ − ω*)   ⇒   τ = Σᵢ rᵢ × jᵢ / Δt

Gravity is already included in V*, so F is the material contact force alone. τ is the material contact torque about the box center of mass. I is frozen at the current pose during the solve.

Return: F [N], τ [N m], V⁺ [m/s], ω⁺ [rad/s], and per-particle impulse −jᵢ [N s]. No predicted future box acceleration is required.

One step: rigid motion → particles → contact force

Input: particle positions and velocity history; body poses and velocities; previous contact wrench; material properties and rigid geometry.

1. Advance the rigid body

Apply gravity and the previous estimated contact force and torque.

Vt+1 = Vt + Δt (g + Ft−1 / m)

Output: updated body pose and linear/angular velocity.

2. Predict all particle positions

The GNN uses neighboring particles and each body's signed distance, normal, and relative surface velocity.

x̂t+1 = xt + Δt (vt + Δt g) + GNN correction

3. Obtain particle velocities

v̂t+1 = (x̂t+1 − xt) / Δt

Optional ablation: project predicted particles outside static rigid geometry, then recompute velocity.

4. Estimate the next body wrench

The analytical contact solver uses predicted particle velocities and the body's state at t.

Ft = Σ Ji / Δt   ·   τt = Σ (ri × Ji) / Δt
Output: next particle/body states and a wrench for the following rigid step. No MPM solve or force network at inference. Live measurements →
