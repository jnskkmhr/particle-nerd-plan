05 / Particle Transolver: Slice Attention & Output

Exchange information through learned slice tokens

Soft assignment from each particle to learned slices

Aᵢₛ = softmaxs(slice logits / temperature)
zₛ = Σᵢ Aᵢₛ valueᵢ / (Σᵢ Aᵢₛ + 0.01)

A slice is a learned group of particle features; it is not a fixed spatial patch. A particle can contribute to several slices.

Attention operates on slice tokens

z′ = Attention(Q(z), K(z), V(z))
particle updateᵢ = Σₛ Aᵢₛ z′ₛ

Each block combines slice attention with residual connections and an MLP. There is no particle-neighbor graph and no N × N particle-attention matrix.

One three-component displacement per particle

Δxᵢ = decoder(hᵢ)   ·   x̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ = xᵢⁿ + Δxᵢ
v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ = Δxᵢ / Δt   ·   Δt = 0.02 s

Every particle is updated. No separate velocity head, region selector, gravity integration, or hard collision projection is applied to particle motion.

Standard Transolver slice attention, adapted locally to particle and collider inputs. Architecture reference. No pretrained crash weights, Transolver++, or distributed-domain attention.
All particles communicate through learned slices, so a short collider influence range does not prevent motion at distant particles. The displacement decoder has a scale of 0.02 m; the linear output is not clipped. Slice attention reduces the attention matrix size but does not make all inference independent of particle count.

07 / Particle Transolver: Active K Selection & Background Anchors

Update nearby sand; retain context from the surrounding bed

N = all particles · K = particles selected for updates · M = background samples supplied only as context.

1 · Select K particles

Rank particles using distance to the intruder and current speed. Nearby or moving particles receive priority. This is a hand-written rule, not a learned selector.

2 · Represent the remaining sand

Add M background samples to the model input. Each valid sample represents several omitted particles. Their predicted motion is discarded.

3 · Approximate stationary support

Give the model a boundary feature for the frozen surrounding sand. Reduce predicted displacement near that boundary; leave unselected particles fixed.

Model input: K + M particles → predict displacement → apply boundary taper → write back only the K selected particles.

08 / Particle Transolver: Active Subset & Boundary Formulation

Three mechanisms, with different jobs

Choose a budget, then rank particles

Input: particle positions and velocities, collider geometry, and material parameters. Output: K particle indices.

  1. Choose K: auto computes a fixed budget from particle count, friction and normalized stiffness. It does not measure prediction error.
  2. Measure actual 3D distance: use signed distance to the foot box / collider surface. “Unclamped” means distances beyond 7 cm remain distinct: 10 cm and 30 cm must not both become 7 cm.
  3. Rank and select: smaller distance and higher speed improve priority. Keep the K lowest scores; this is a budget, not a guarantee that every moving particle is selected.
score = (surface gap − selection radius)
− 0.8 × max(speed − speed threshold, 0)

The radius and speed threshold are heuristic material-dependent values. The coefficient 0.8 converts speed into a distance preference. See the next slide tab for what happens to omitted particles.

Let a few samples represent many omitted particles

Input: K selected particles and M samples spread across the original particle indices. Output: a smaller, weighted input to Transolver.

The blue points are existing sand particles, not new particles or rigid constraints. Valid background samples receive a larger weight when Transolver forms its slice summaries.

Selected particle weight = 1
Background sample weight = (N − K) / M_valid

Example: omit 800 particles and retain 8 valid samples. Each sample contributes with weight 100 to the slice numerator and denominator.

Samples overlapping the selected set are normally masked out. Their motion predictions are not written back.

“Quadrature” means approximating a sum using weighted samples. This preserves the represented particle count, not the exact full-bed features or prediction.

Describe support from sand that we no longer update

Problem: freezing surrounding sand removes its motion from the prediction. Sparse background samples alone do not enforce local contact with it.

  1. Construct an approximate boundary around the foot’s selected region, with a normal pointing inward and biased upward.
  2. Supply boundary features: distance, normal, stationary-boundary relative velocity (−particle velocity), and material friction.
  3. Taper the output: reduce displacement toward the edge and suppress motion into the approximate boundary.
floor_slot = the model-input channel normally used for rigid-ground features

For selected particles, replace this channel only when the synthetic boundary influence exceeds the actual ground influence. The simulator’s ground geometry is unchanged.

The “cup” is a heuristic feature and displacement correction—not a physical cup, a new rigid collider, or an exact stress/contact solve.

09 / Particle Transolver: Continuum Packing Incompressibility

Preventing particles from coalescing onto identical points

Because slice attention has no local neighbor graph, nearby particles at similar coordinates receive nearly identical decoded displacements Δxᵢ. A differentiable 2-iteration continuum packing step preserves minimum grain spacing.

1 · Point-Cloud Coalescence

Smooth neural fields map identical positions to identical displacements; once two particles overlap, they never separate on their own.

2 · Upward Confining Cup Normal

The confining boundary normal n_cup = normalize(clamp_z(−n_foot, min=+0.25)) always supports active particles upward from the stationary bed.

3 · Pair Packing Repulsion

2-iteration pairwise repulsion (enforce_packing_incompressibility) enforces d_ij ≥ d_min and floor non-penetration across the active set K.

δxᵢ = k_pack ∑_{d_ij < d_min} (d_min − d_ij) · w_up(z_i − z_j) · w_shell(τ_j) · (x_i − x_j)/d_ij  ·  Maintains 15.4 mm median spacing (0 pairs < 5 mm) over 20 s.
Implemented in examples/particle_nerd/g1/data.py.

Velocity gradient G

Fit Gᵢ from neighbors within 6.5 cm at every material particle.

Candidate contacts

SDF at xᵢⁿ → distance dᵢ, outward normal nᵢ, surface point pᵢ.

dᵢ ≤ Δt max(nᵢ · (vshovel,free(pᵢ) − uᵢ), 0)

Free surface velocity: after gravity and external loads, before material impulse.

Impulse solve and wrench

12 / Contact Force Estimation: Unilateral Coulomb Formulation

Contact forces for a box or an actuated robot

Algorithm 1 · One coupled timestep, n → n+1

InputCurrent particle positions xᵢⁿ, velocities vᵢⁿ and history; rigid-object configuration qⁿ and base/joint velocity Vgenn; masses, material parameters, geometry, known loads and timestep Δt.OutputNext particle positions and velocities; next rigid-object configuration qⁿ⁺¹ and velocity Vgenn+1; material force and torque on each rigid body.

Notation: lowercase v = material velocity; uppercase V = rigid-object velocity. k = contact iteration; * = free motion; hat = particle NeRD prediction. All iterations belong to the same timestep n → n+1.

1Predict particle motionRun particle NeRD using the current state and history → predicted x̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ and v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹.
2Compute free object motionApply actuation, passive forces, known external loads and gravity → free velocity Vgenn+1,*.
3Prepare contact quantitiesUse geometry at qⁿ. Extrapolate predicted material velocities to rigid-surface contact points.
4Initialize contact solveSet Vgenn+1,0 = Vgenn+1,*; set accumulated material-on-body contact impulses jᵢ0 = 0.
5Repeat k = 0, …, K−1 (K: maximum contact iterations); stop early on convergence:
6Compute relative velocitiesUse Vgenn+1,k and auxiliary material velocities, including the effective material-mass response.
7Update contact stateEnforce compressive contact and friction; update jᵢk+1 and the shared velocity Vgenn+1,k+1.
8Reconstruct body loadsAfter the loop, sum final contact impulses and their moments; divide by Δt to obtain force and torque.
9Advance the rigid objectsIntegrate from qⁿ, Vgenn with known loads and reconstructed contact loads → qⁿ⁺¹, Vgenn+1.

Particle NeRD runs once per timestep. Only the contact solve iterates. Its temporary velocities are not copied into particle or rigid-body states.

1. Include actuation in free motion

Known loads determine free motion before material contact.

τactGeneralized actuator force after actuator dynamics and transmission. τpassiveGeneralized force from passive elements, such as joint springs and viscous damping. τexternalKnown applied loads, such as a user’s push or drag, mapped to generalized coordinates. Excludes actuation, passive forces, gravity and the unknown material reaction.

qⁿ: current robot configuration. M(qⁿ): robot mass matrix. b(qⁿ, Vgenn): gravity, Coriolis and centrifugal bias forces. These forces act on base and joint degrees of freedom.

Vgenn: current robot velocity. Vgenn+1,*: free robot velocity after timestep Δt. τknown: sum of the three forces above.

τknown = τact + τpassive + τexternal
Vgenn+1,* = Vgenn + Δt M(qⁿ)⁻¹ [τknown − b(qⁿ, Vgenn)]
Explicit step shown. Use MJWarp’s free velocity directly when available; do not integrate known loads twice.

2. Evaluate both velocities at the contact point

A. Material velocity evaluated at the rigid surface

xᵢⁿ; sᵢCurrent particle position; nearest point on the rigid-body surface. v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹Next particle velocity supplied by particle NeRD using current state and history. Gᵢ3 × 3 spatial gradient of predicted particle velocity, fitted from neighboring particles by weighted least squares. Units: 1/s. v̂surface,in+1Predicted material velocity evaluated at the surface point sᵢ.
v̂surface,in+1 = v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ + Gᵢ(sᵢ − xᵢⁿ)

Gᵢ times the position offset adjusts particle velocity to the surface location. This is spatial extrapolation, not another time integration.

B. Rigid-body velocity at that same surface point

qⁿ; Vgenn+1,kCurrent configuration; temporary next-step robot velocity at contact iteration k. Jᵢ(qⁿ)3 × Ndof point Jacobian. It maps the robot’s Ndof velocity components to world velocity at sᵢ. Vsurface,in+1,kTemporary next-step velocity of the rigid-body point at sᵢ, at iteration k.
Vsurface,in+1,k = Jᵢ(qⁿ)Vgenn+1,k

For a free box, VCOMn+1,k is center-of-mass velocity, ω n+1,k is the temporary angular velocity, c is COM position, and rᵢ = sᵢ − c is the contact arm.

Vsurface,in+1,k = VCOMn+1,k + ωn+1,k × rᵢ

Initialization: Vgenn+1,0 = Vgenn+1,*. Step 3 updates k; Vgenn is not used as the initial contact velocity.

C. Compare two different objects at one location

Vsurface,in+1,k: rigid-body point velocity.
v̂surface,in+1: material velocity extrapolated to the same point.

VCOMn and vᵢⁿ are different states. The first belongs to the rigid body’s center of mass; the second belongs to particle i. Neither determines the other.

Contact impulses respond to their relative motion. Step 3 includes the effective material response before computing the final relative velocity.

All linear velocities here are in world coordinates, in m/s. A stationary, rotating box can have zero COM velocity and nonzero surface velocity.

3. Solve individual contact impulses

A. Initialize once per timestep

Vgenn+1,*: free robot velocity from Step 1. Vgenn+1,k: next-step robot velocity estimate at iteration k. jᵢk: accumulated material-on-body impulse at iteration k, over n → n+1 (N·s).

mᵢ: particle mass. κ: dimensionless effective-mass multiplier. mᵢeff: assumed material mass responding locally at contact i.

Vgenn+1,0 = Vgenn+1,*  ;   jᵢ0 = 0  ;   mᵢeff = κmᵢ
All robot contacts share Vgenn+1,k. An impulse at one foot can change another foot’s velocity through the robot mass matrix. The box estimator defaults to κ = 10.

B. Compute relative velocity after the accumulated impulses

vaux,in+1,k: auxiliary material velocity after impulse −jᵢk. wᵢk: rigid-body surface velocity minus auxiliary material velocity. M = M(qⁿ) and Jᵢ = Jᵢ(qⁿ) stay fixed during the iterations.

vaux,in+1,k = v̂surface,in+1 − jᵢk / mᵢeff
Vgenn+1,k = Vgenn+1,* + M⁻¹ Σⱼ Jⱼᵀ jⱼk
wᵢk = Vsurface,in+1,k − vaux,in+1,k = JᵢVgenn+1,k − vaux,in+1,k
The term jᵢk/mᵢeff retains effective material mass. Auxiliary velocities are not written back to the particle NeRD state.

C. Compute each contact’s impulse update

jᵢk: 3D impulse at contact i, iteration k. nᵢ: unit normal, material → body. pᵢk = nᵢ · jᵢk: normal impulse. Uppercase Jᵢ is the robot Jacobian.

wᵢk: body-surface velocity minus auxiliary material velocity. Kᵢ: normal response per unit impulse. dᵢ: signed gap (negative inside). γᵢ: gap correction speed; β = 0.01: penetration correction factor.

Kᵢ = nᵢᵀJᵢM⁻¹Jᵢᵀnᵢ + 1/mᵢeff
γᵢ = [max(dᵢ, 0) + β min(dᵢ, 0)] / Δt
pᵢk,candidate = max(0, pᵢk − (nᵢ · wᵢk + γᵢ)/Kᵢ)
Correct normal approach while keeping the normal impulse nonnegative. Separation can require zero impulse; the two velocities need not always match.

Bᵢ: 3 × 2 matrix of orthonormal tangent directions. zᵢk: two tangential components of jᵢk. Aᵢ: 3D relative-velocity response per impulse. Hᵢ: its 2 × 2 tangential response. I₃ is the 3 × 3 identity.

zᵢk = Bᵢᵀjᵢk  ;   Aᵢ = JᵢM⁻¹Jᵢᵀ + I₃/mᵢeff
Hᵢ = BᵢᵀAᵢBᵢ

wᵢk,N: relative velocity after applying only this contact’s proposed normal change. hᵢk: two-component right-hand side for the friction solve.

wᵢk,N = wᵢk + Aᵢnᵢ(pᵢk,candidate − pᵢk)
hᵢk = Hᵢzᵢk − Bᵢᵀwᵢk,N

The normal change can affect tangential velocity through rigid-body rotation and articulation. Other contacts remain at iteration k during this local calculation.

μ: friction coefficient. z: trial two-component tangential impulse. zᵢk,candidate: its minimizing value. Hᵢ and hᵢk are defined in “Tangent response.”

zᵢk,candidate = argminz [½ zᵀHᵢz − (hᵢk)ᵀz]
subject to ‖z‖ ≤ μpᵢk,candidate

zᵢk,free: unconstrained solution. λᵢk ≥ 0: scalar chosen to satisfy the friction limit when sliding. I₂ is the 2 × 2 identity.

zᵢk,free = Hᵢ⁻¹hᵢk
Inside the limit: zᵢk,candidate = zᵢk,free
Outside: zᵢk,candidate = (Hᵢ + λᵢkI₂)⁻¹hᵢk,   ‖zᵢk,candidate‖ = μpᵢk,candidate

Zero friction radius gives zero tangential impulse. Sticking can cancel tangential slip; sliding saturates the limit. This matches the implementation’s friction-disk solve.

jᵢk,candidate: proposed 3D impulse in world coordinates. α: relaxation (box default 0.1). Δjᵢk: change in accumulated impulse. Bᵢ converts the two tangent components back to a 3D vector.

jᵢk,candidate = pᵢk,candidatenᵢ + Bᵢzᵢk,candidate
Δjᵢk = α(jᵢk,candidate − jᵢk)
jᵢk+1 = jᵢk + Δjᵢk
Step D combines all contact increments into the shared rigid-object velocity. Repeat: relative velocity → normal update → friction solve → body response.

Particle NeRD’s supplied velocity stays fixed during the solve. The auxiliary material velocity changes with the opposite impulse through effective material mass. No auxiliary correction is written back to the particles.

D. Update rigid-object velocity; repeat B → C → D

Δjᵢk = jᵢk+1 − jᵢk: change in accumulated impulse. Combine all contact increments to update the shared rigid-object velocity.

Vgenn+1,k+1 = Vgenn+1,k + M⁻¹ Σᵢ Jᵢᵀ Δjᵢk
Return to B with the updated velocity. Recompute relative velocities, update contact impulses in C, then update rigid-object velocity here in D.

Jacobi reference / final verification: exit the B → C → D loop when the impulse-correction residual meets tolerance or after K iterations. Continue to Step 4 to convert final impulses into forces and torques.

4. Convert contact impulses to rigid-body loads

If the solve converges early, retain that impulse as jᵢK.
τcontact = Σᵢ JᵢᵀjᵢK / Δt
Flink = Σi on link jᵢK / Δt  ;   Tlink = Σi on link (rᵢ × jᵢK) / Δt
Supply either generalized force or per-link force and torque. They describe the same load; do not apply both.

The box runner passes force and COM torque to MJWarp, which advances the actual rigid-body state.

Vgenn: current state. Vgenn+1,*: free next-step velocity. Vgenn+1,0 = Vgenn+1,*. Vgenn+1,k = Vgenn+1,* + M⁻¹ΣᵢJᵢᵀjᵢk. Vsurface,in+1,k = JᵢVgenn+1,k. vaux,in+1,k = v̂surface,in+1 − jᵢk/mᵢeff. Geometry and Jacobians remain evaluated at qⁿ.
Full articulated extension with effective material mass. Generalized dynamics reference.
This derives the articulated extension of the effective-material-mass contact model; it is not a claim that the current estimator implements full robot coupling. qⁿ, M and contact Jacobians are frozen during each solve. M⁻¹ denotes a linear solve, not an explicitly formed inverse. The free-velocity equation illustrates explicit integration. G_i is defined as velocity-component derivatives with respect to position. The Warp fit_gradients kernel stores its transpose, and prepare_contacts transposes the stored matrix before extrapolation. The estimator accepts next-step particle velocity supplied by particle NeRD; the contact formulation does not prescribe the motion predictor’s architecture. An implementation must use a response consistent with its integrator, including implicit damping or actuation if present. Active joint limits, loop constraints and rigid-ground contacts require additional constraint equations or a constraint-consistent response; an unconstrained M⁻¹ alone does not account for them. The current G1 estimator instead uses independent virtual-foot velocities and inertias prepared by the coupler. Actuation and gravity are already included in those prepared velocities. The finite material response D is an assumed local, independent-contact approximation, not a learned or measured constitutive law. It is retained in this derivation and in the default box implementation.
13 / Contact Force Estimation: Effective Material Mass

Effective mass supplies the missing contact response

jᵢ: material-on-body impulse. mᵢ: particle mass. κ: effective-mass multiplier. Δvaux,i: assumed local material-velocity change in the contact solve.

Impulse on body: +jᵢ   ·   impulse on material: −jᵢ
Assumed local velocity change: Δvaux,i = −jᵢ / (κ mᵢ)

What this equation supplies

A relation between the unknown impulse and the material’s response. Together with body inertia and contact constraints, it determines the iterative contact update.

What κ means

κ scales particle mass in this response. Larger κ gives a smaller local velocity change for the same impulse. It approximates resistance from surrounding material.

This is an auxiliary contact model, not a second particle update. Particle NeRD already predicts particle motion including interaction; applying −jᵢ again to its stored velocity would count contact twice.

κ is an estimator parameter, not a measured material law. Current defaults: κ = 10 for the unified box runner; κ = 180 for the shovel runner; κ = 100 for the published G1 filtering benchmark.

DVerify with p12’s solverStart from these impulses; check and refine using the original impulse residual.
5Convert loads + advanceSum impulses and moments, divide by Δt, and advance the rigid body — same as p12.

Why faster: p16 iteratively adjusts every contact impulse. Here, contact impulses are functions of only six shared body-velocity unknowns. Every contact is still evaluated.

1. Compute free body velocity — same as p12

VgennCurrent rigid-body velocity: three COM linear components and three angular components.Vgenn+1,*Predicted next body velocity before the unknown material reaction.qⁿ; ΔtCurrent body pose; simulation timestep.M(qⁿ)6 × 6 rigid-body mass matrix, including rotational inertia.τknownKnown applied force and torque, including the user’s drag.b(qⁿ, Vgenn)Gravity and gyroscopic bias, using p12’s sign convention.
Vgenn+1,* = Vgenn + Δt M(qⁿ)⁻¹[τknown − b(qⁿ, Vgenn)]
The optimization starts after free motion. This explicit formula illustrates the inputs; use the prepared free velocity when the rigid solver already provides it.

2. Prepare material velocities — same as p12

xᵢⁿ; sᵢCurrent particle position; nearest point on the rigid surface at pose qⁿ.v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹Next particle velocity predicted by particle NeRD from the current state and history.Gᵢ3 × 3 spatial gradient of predicted particle velocity, fitted from neighboring particles (1/s).v̂surface,in+1Predicted material velocity extrapolated to surface point sᵢ.Jᵢ = Jᵢ(qⁿ)3 × 6 Jacobian mapping body velocity to world velocity at sᵢ.
v̂surface,in+1 = v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ + Gᵢ(sᵢ − xᵢⁿ)
These material velocities and contact geometry stay fixed during Step 3. The speedup comes from the contact solve; it does not replace the Gᵢ fit or particle NeRD.

3. Solve body velocity, then verify contact impulses

A. Express each impulse using a trial body velocity

Vgenn+1,kTemporary next body velocity at nonlinear iteration k. Initialize k = 0 with Vgenn+1,*.jᵢk; mᵢeffMaterial-on-body contact impulse; effective material mass κmᵢ, with particle mass mᵢ and multiplier κ.vaux,in+1,kAuxiliary material surface velocity after the opposite impulse −jᵢᵏ.eᵢk; wᵢkBody–material velocity difference before / after the auxiliary material response.
eᵢk = JᵢVgenn+1,k − v̂surface,in+1
vaux,in+1,k = v̂surface,in+1 − jᵢk/mᵢeff
wᵢk = eᵢk + jᵢk/mᵢeff
At a fixed trial body velocity, each contact responds independently through its effective material mass. This lets us calculate its impulse directly (B).

B. Calculate normal impulse and tangential friction

nᵢ; μUnit normal from material toward the body; contact friction coefficient.dᵢ; β; γᵢSigned particle-to-body gap (negative inside); penetration factor; gap correction velocity.pᵢk; tᵢkScalar normal impulse; 3D tangential impulse. eᵢᵏ is the velocity difference defined in A.tᵢk,freeTangential impulse that would cancel slip without the friction limit.
γᵢ = [max(dᵢ, 0) + β min(dᵢ, 0)] / Δt
pᵢk = max(0, −mᵢeff(nᵢ · eᵢk + γᵢ))
tᵢk,free = −mᵢeff[eᵢk − (nᵢ · eᵢk)nᵢ]
tᵢk = min(1, μpᵢk / ‖tᵢk,free‖) tᵢk,free
jᵢk = pᵢknᵢ + tᵢk

If tᵢk,free = 0, set tᵢk = 0. Body velocity is fixed here; body inertia enters in C.

C. Solve six coupled momentum-balance equations

RkSix-component mismatch between the trial body velocity and the velocity produced by all contact impulses.Dk6 × 6 derivative of that mismatch with respect to the trial body velocity.δVk; ηkBody-velocity correction; step length reduced until the mismatch decreases.
Rk = Vgenn+1,k − Vgenn+1,* − M⁻¹ΣᵢJᵢᵀjᵢk
Dk δVk = −Rk
Vgenn+1,k+1 = Vgenn+1,k + ηk δVk
Repeat A → B → C with the updated trial velocity. The derivative accounts for how all contact impulses change with body motion. This is a numerical Newton method implemented in Warp, separate from the Newton simulator.

M and Jᵢ remain fixed at qⁿ. Current implementation: at most 30 nonlinear iterations; backtracking tests up to 16 step lengths.

D. Verify using the original contact equations

Use the impulses from A–C to initialize p12’s contact solver. Reconstruct the temporary body velocity from those same impulses so the initial state satisfies momentum balance.

jᵢinitImpulse supplied by the body-velocity solve.Vgenn+1,initConsistent body velocity used to start the verification iterations.
Vgenn+1,init = Vgenn+1,* + M⁻¹ΣᵢJᵢᵀjᵢinit
Run p12’s impulse updates until the original residual reaches 10⁻⁹, or its iteration limit. If the body-velocity initialization fails, start the original solver from zero impulses.

This check preserves the original convergence criterion. It verifies the same contact approximation; it does not validate that approximation against MPM.

4. Convert impulses to force and torque — same as p12

jᵢK; KFinal verified impulse; maximum verification iterations. Retain the converged impulse if stopping early.rᵢ = sᵢ − cⁿContact arm from the body COM cⁿ to surface point sᵢ.Fbody; TbodyMaterial force on the body; material torque about its COM, both in world coordinates.ΔtSimulation interval over which the contact impulse acts.
Fbody = ΣᵢjᵢK / Δt
Tbody = Σᵢ(rᵢ × jᵢK) / Δt
Pass these loads to MJWarp to advance the actual body state. The temporary body velocities and auxiliary material velocities are not copied into simulation states.

15 / Contact Force Estimation: RFT-Anisotropic Mobility

Granular RFT directional effective mass and compliant tangential slip

Algorithm 3 · Same 6-DOF solver as p14, with RFT directional & tangential mobility

InputSame as p14: current particle/body state, predicted velocity v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹, geometry, known loads, Δt — no neural force head.OutputMaterial-on-body force Fbody and COM torque Tbody, without p14’s horizontal drag spikes or particle sticking.

What changed from p14: p14 uses one isotropic scalar κ = 180 for normal support, horizontal plowing, and tangential slip. Here, normal mobility depends on contact orientation nz,i (Granular RFT) and tangential mobility is decoupled (κt ≪ κvert).

1Predict particle motionParticle NeRD predicts next positions and velocities v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ = Δxᵢ / Δt — unchanged from p12 & p14.
2Free motion + contactsCompute free body velocity Vgenn+1,* and extrapolated material surface velocities v̂surface,in+1 — unchanged from p14.
3Modify p14’s local contact response (Steps 3A–3D):
ARFT directional massReplace isotropic κ mᵢ with normal mn,ieff(nz,i) = κn,i(nz,i) mᵢ and decoupled tangential mt,ieff = κt mᵢ.
BCalculate contact impulsesCompute normal impulse pᵢk using mn,ieff and tangential friction tᵢk using mt,ieff (still inside ‖tᵢk‖ ≤ μ pᵢk).
CCorrect body velocitySolve the same 6 × 6 momentum-balance system Dk δVk = −Rk with the anisotropic Jacobian.
DVerify with anisotropic KᵢVerify to 10⁻⁹ impulse residual using diagonal slack diag(1/mn,ieff, 1/mt,ieff, 1/mt,ieff).
5Convert loads + advanceSum impulses and moments, divide by Δt, and advance the rigid body in MJWarp — same as p12 & p14.

Why physics-integrated RFT instead of an NN force head: 100% analytical momentum conservation and Coulomb cone bounds, zero OOD hallucination, and 4.6× lower horizontal drag error than p14.

1. Why p14 over-estimates drag & feels “sticky”

Double-counted ΔvᵢTransolver runs before the force solve and predicts v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ = Δxᵢ/Δt, which already includes the shovel’s push on the sand.Isotropic κ = 180To support the 20.5 kg shovel vertically (201 N), p14 sets κ = 180. Each particle then acts as 180 × 0.78 g = 0.14 kg (80 contacts = 11.2 kg).Horizontal wallDuring plowing, if predicted v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ lags the blade by just 0.1 m/s, p14 multiplies that lag by κ = 180 across 80 contacts → 740 N drag spike (187.2 N XY RMSE, 98.9% of p14’s error!).Sticky tangent PDp14 uses the same κ = 180 to drive tangential slip et,ik → 0, welding sand to the blade instead of letting it flow.
p14 flaw: pᵢk = max(0, −κ mᵢ(nᵢ · eᵢk + γᵢ))  and  tᵢk,free = −κ mᵢ et,ik  with  κ = 180 everywhere
Result in p14: particles act like a stiff PD damper glued to the shovel, hindering horizontal and rotational motion.

2. Granular RFT directional effective mass & slip compliance

nᵢ = (nx,i, ny,i, nz,i)Unit normal from material toward the rigid body at surface point sᵢ.κvert = 220; p = 3Vertical bearing multiplier when body pushes downward on sand (nz,i > 0, confined by rigid ground).κhoriz = 8; κup = 2Horizontal plowing multiplier (nz,i ≈ 0) and upward-surface multiplier (nz,i < 0), where sand yields plastically toward the free surface.κt = 4Decoupled tangential slip multiplier (κt ≪ κvert) so particles slide along the blade instead of welding.
κn,i(nz,i) = κvert max(nz,i, 0)p + κhoriz(1 − nz,i²) + κup max(−nz,i, 0)
mn,ieff = κn,i(nz,i) mᵢ   ·   mt,ieff = κt mᵢ
Setting κhoriz = κup = κt = κvert and p = 1 recovers p14 exactly. No neural weights are used.

3. Solve body velocity with anisotropic mobility (diff from p14)

A. Separate normal and tangential material mobilities (vs p14 Step 3A)

eᵢk = (en,ik, et,ik)Body–material velocity difference at trial body velocity Vgenn+1,k, split into normal and tangent components.mn,ieff; mt,ieffDirectional normal mass κn,i(nz,i)mᵢ and tangential mass κtmᵢ (replaces p14’s single scalar mᵢeff).wn,ik; wt,ikPost-impulse normal and tangential relative velocities.
eᵢk = JᵢVgenn+1,k − v̂surface,in+1
wn,ik = en,ik + pᵢk / mn,ieff   ·   wt,ik = et,ik + tᵢk / mt,ieff
In p14, both equations divided by the same κ mᵢ. Separating mn,ieff and mt,ieff keeps each contact closed-form at fixed Vgenn+1,k.

B. Normal impulse & compliant Coulomb friction (vs p14 Step 3B)

pᵢk ≥ 0Unilateral normal impulse computed with directional mn,ieff(nz,i).tᵢk,freeUncapped tangential impulse computed with compliant mt,ieff = κt mᵢ (45× softer than p14).tᵢk; jᵢkTangential impulse projected onto the Coulomb disk of radius μ pᵢk; total impulse.
γᵢ = [max(dᵢ, 0) + β min(dᵢ, 0)] / Δt
pᵢk = max(0, −mn,ieff(nᵢ · eᵢk + γᵢ))
tᵢk,free = −mt,ieff[eᵢk − (nᵢ · eᵢk)nᵢ]
tᵢk = min(1, μpᵢk / ‖tᵢk,free‖) tᵢk,free   ·   jᵢk = pᵢknᵢ + tᵢk

When sliding (‖tᵢk,free‖ > μpᵢk), ∂tᵢk/∂en,ik scales with −μ mn,ieff while ∂tᵢk/∂et,ik scales with −mt,ieff.

C. Solve six coupled momentum-balance equations (vs p14 Step 3C)

RkSix-component momentum residual (identical definition to p14).∂jᵢk/∂eᵢk3 × 3 local contact Jacobian in contact frame: diagonal (−mn,ieff, −mt,ieff, −mt,ieff) in sticking, rank-2 + normal coupling in sliding.Dk = I − M⁻¹Σᵢ Jᵢᵀ(∂jᵢk/∂eᵢk)JᵢExact 6 × 6 Newton Jacobian with backtracking line search.
Rk = Vgenn+1,k − Vgenn+1,* − M⁻¹ΣᵢJᵢᵀjᵢk
Dk δVk = −Rk   ·   Vgenn+1,k+1 = Vgenn+1,k + ηk δVk
Implemented in force_estimation/body_velocity.py and particle_nerd_batch/force/body_velocity.py. Converges to 10⁻¹² in 5–7 Newton iterations.

D. Verify with matching anisotropic compliance Kᵢ (vs p14 Step 3D)

In kernels.py, the per-contact compliance matrix Kᵢ and Jacobi verification use the exact same normal and tangential slacks so Step 3C’s fixed point matches Step 3D’s fixed point:

Kᵢ3 × 3 contact-frame compliance matrix combining rigid-body mobility and diagonal material slack.
Kᵢ = Fᵢ M⁻¹ Fᵢᵀ + diag(1 / mn,ieff, 1 / mt,ieff, 1 / mt,ieff)
Because Kᵢ uses the identical (mn,ieff, mt,ieff) as the Newton initializer, verification finishes in 1 iteration at 10⁻⁹ tolerance.

4. Convert impulses to force and torque — same as p12 & p14

jᵢK; rᵢ = sᵢ − cⁿVerified RFT contact impulse; exact lever arm from body COM cⁿ to surface point sᵢ.Fbody; TbodyMaterial-on-body world force and world torque about COM, applied across the four 5 ms MJWarp substeps.CLI flagsUse --contact-solver rft_body_velocity in play_shovel.py or --force-model rft in particle_nerd_batch.
Fbody = ΣᵢjᵢK / Δt   ·   Tbody = Σᵢ(rᵢ × jᵢK) / Δt
Unlike black-box NN force predictors, this solver is strictly passive: it can never inject energy or hallucinate forces outside the training distribution.
