14 / EQUATIONS OF MOTION ONE-DIMENSIONAL EXAMPLE

Equations link the rigid body, particles, and ground

Upward is positive. Each contact force appears with opposite signs on the two objects it connects.

Rigid boxmb ab = U + B − Wb
Particle 1m₁ a₁ = S₁₂ − B − W₁
Particle 2m₂ a₂ = S₂₃ − S₁₂ − W₂
Particle 3m₃ a₃ = G − S₂₃ − W₃
Fixed ground0 = R − G − Wg

m = mass; a = vertical acceleration; W = weight (m g).
U: external actuator force on the box. R: external support force on the ground. Both are signed upward.

Add all five equations:mb ab + m₁ a₁ + m₂ a₂ + m₃ a₃ = U + R − Σi mi g
Contact forces cancel in the sum. The actuator force U and support force R remain.
With known U and measured box acceleration, B follows from the box equation. With prescribed box motion and unknown U, that equation introduces another unknown.
Sum over the box, particles 1–3, and supported ground slab. Vertical translation only; zero ground acceleration does not imply zero contact force.
This schematic extends the following three-particle example to include both rigid objects. The box can move vertically; the ground slab is fixed by an external support. B is the magnitude of the upward force of particle 1 on the box and the downward force of the box on particle 1. S12 is upward on particle 1 and downward on particle 2. S23 is upward on particle 2 and downward on particle 3. G is upward on particle 3 and downward on the ground. U is the signed net external actuator or holding force on the box; R is the signed external force holding the ground slab fixed. Weights are positive magnitudes acting downward. There are no other forces in this example. Summing the five equations cancels B, S12, S23, and G. This removes internal forces from the total balance but also removes the contact forces we want to reconstruct. For specified accelerations and known weights, the six unknowns are B, S12, S23, G, U, R. The five independent balances admit the variation: increase B, S12, S23, G, and R by the same amount, and decrease U by that amount. Every acceleration remains unchanged. This corresponds to the next slide's stationary example with a stronger downward holding force on the box and a stronger upward ground support. If U is independently known and the box acceleration is measured, the box equation instead determines its net contact force in this simple chain. In a forward coupled simulation the next box acceleration is itself an output, so one cannot assume it is independently known to close reconstruction. In three dimensions include vector force balances and rigid-body moment balances, with actuator, joint, and support reactions represented explicitly. This is an illustrative equation chain, not the completed uniqueness analysis for the recorded MPM scene. No MPM solve is introduced at inference.

Different contact forces can produce identical motion

Three stationary particles. Only particle 1 touches the box; only particle 3 touches the ground.

Every particle equation is satisfied in both cases. Adding the equations gives ground force − box force = 30 N in either case.
Upward force is positive. Equal-and-opposite internal forces cancel. Identical geometry and zero acceleration do not select A or B.
This example uses force balance and compressive contact only; a material law or a known boundary load could provide the missing information.
This is an exact one-dimensional counterexample, not simulation data. The three particles each weigh 10 N and have zero acceleration. Let B be the downward box force, S12 and S23 be compressive internal forces, and G be the upward ground force. The individual balances are S12-B-10=0, S23-S12-10=0, G-S23-10=0. Thus S12=B+10, S23=B+20, G=B+30. There are three independent scalar equations for four unknown force magnitudes. Any nonnegative B gives a compressive solution. Adding 100 N to all four unknowns changes the box and ground reactions while leaving every particle equation unchanged. The change (100,100,100,100) is a null-space direction of the balance matrix and affects the body reactions. Known contact locations and internal action-reaction are already included. This is stronger than showing ambiguity only in the summed system. It does not prove that every three-dimensional particle scene has ambiguous body reactions; that must be checked for the actual geometry and constraints. It also does not claim that both states satisfy a specified elastic or granular constitutive law with identical history. Such a law is exactly the additional information omitted by force balance alone. The fixed box has an unmeasured holding reaction, so its own equilibrium does not provide a known applied load. Body reactions are opposite to the body-on-particle forces illustrated here.
16 / REACTION IDENTIFIABILITY MATHEMATICAL CRITERION

Check whether the equations determine each body reaction

1. Assemble all balancesBalance each particle’s forces. Internal forces have opposite signs on neighbors. Include contact forces only where contact occurs.
2. Look for force changesCan forces change while every balance remains satisfied and contact constraints remain valid?
3. Check body reactionsIf those changes alter a body’s net force or torque, motion alone has not determined that reaction.

Some ambiguity is acceptable

Internal forces may be nonunique while every body reaction is unchanged. We only need the body reactions to be determined.

Otherwise, identify missing information

Examples: a material force–deformation relationship, material history, or a known applied boundary load. Test whether it resolves the ambiguity.

What additional equations must provide

A material law or contact-response model must constrain force changes that would otherwise alter the reconstructed body reaction.

Inference requirement: NeRD particle motion + nonlearned reaction reconstruction. No MPM solve at inference.
MPM is used only to generate reference data and evaluate results.
For the full particle system, write A f = b, where f contains internal-force and contact-force unknowns and b contains known inertial loads after subtracting gravity and other known external loads. Let R f collect per-body forces and moments. If an admissible perturbation d satisfies A d = 0 but R d is nonzero, body reactions are not uniquely determined by the balance equations. A null-space direction that changes only internal forces is harmless for the reaction output. Inequality constraints matter: a linear null-space direction establishes feasible ambiguity only when a nonzero perturbation remains inside compression, friction, and contact conditions. In larger systems one can bound each reaction component over the feasible force set rather than assume that all rank deficiency affects body reactions. Net torque and angular-momentum constraints must be included consistently with the selected particle state and internal-force model. The actual full-scene analysis has not been performed yet. Known material parameters are useful only when explicit material equations use them. For the cable analogy, known connectivity and a rod law add structure; arbitrary particle-neighborhood edges do not provide the same information. The user explicitly excludes any MPM solve at inference. Mathematical ideas from continuum mechanics may inform a separate reconstruction, but inference must not retain or reintroduce the MPM update. No new numerical experiment or implementation is claimed by these slides.
17 / DISCRETE ELEMENT METHOD SOURCE OF ADDITIONAL EQUATIONS

Relate each contact force to deformation and motion

A simple noncohesive DEM contact law supplies force values from overlap, relative velocity, and contact history.

Normal force magnitude · active contact

Fn = max(0, kn δ + cn vclose)

Elastic compression + damping; closing speed is positive.
Tangential force vector · before sliding

Ft,trial = −kt ξ − ct vt

ξ accumulates relative tangential displacement during contact.
Sliding limit

‖Ft‖ ≤ μ Fn

Cap the trial force and update history when sliding occurs.
Additional equations: force is related to contact deformation and history, rather than selected only by a small-force preference. Separated particles carry no contact force.
k: contact stiffness; c: damping coefficient; μ: contact friction. Ground and moving bodies use the same law with their own geometry and surface velocity.
Source: LAMMPS granular contact laws and particle–wall contacts. The equations show a linear spring–damper model; Hertz–Mindlin is an alternative.
DEM means discrete element method. The equations illustrate one noncohesive linear spring–dashpot model, not all DEM formulations. For positive overlap, the normal force is the positive part of stiffness times overlap plus damping times signed closing speed. Use zero force after separation; do not apply a damping force across a gap. Tangential velocity is measured at the contact point relative to the other particle or rigid surface, including rotational contributions where represented. Transport tangential history into the changing contact plane, update it over the contact interval, correct it when the Coulomb bound is reached, and reset it when contact is lost. Static friction may remain nonzero at zero instantaneous tangential velocity because of stored tangential displacement. The force on the other object is equal and opposite. Particle–particle contacts can use the same law structure with appropriate radii and contact parameters. Hertz–Mindlin models provide nonlinear normal and tangential relationships involving elastic parameters and contact geometry. These are physical model parameters, so nonlearned does not mean parameter-free. This slide explains the source equations only; it does not claim their direct applicability to Newton MPM material points. References: https://docs.lammps.org/pair_granular.html ; https://docs.lammps.org/fix_wall_gran.html ; https://docs.lammps.org/Howto_granular.html .

19 / CONTACT-FORCE RECONSTRUCTION IMPLEMENTED APPROXIMATION

From particle motion to force on the rigid box

Inputs and outputs of one force-estimation step

INPUT · particles

Current positions xᵢⁿ, masses mᵢ, and predicted next velocities v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹.

INPUT · rigid box

Current center cⁿ, orientation Rⁿ, linear velocity Vⁿ, angular velocity ωⁿ; mass m, inertia I, and box size.

OUTPUT

Material-on-box force F and torque τ; contact-corrected box velocities V⁺, ω⁺ and per-particle impulse −jᵢ.

Also supplied: Δt, gravity g, friction μ, contact offset δ, effective mass scale κ, penetration coefficient β; previous impulses if history is enabled.

This routine estimates contact loads. Particle positions and box pose are held fixed inside it. Newton advances the rigid-body pose separately.

1. Compute the box velocity without contact

Keep the current box pose fixed. Integrate gravity and the rigid body’s gyroscopic term for one timestep.

V* = Vⁿ + Δt g
ω* = ωⁿ − Δt I⁻¹ [ωⁿ × (I ωⁿ)]

I = Rⁿ Ilocal (Rⁿ)ᵀ is inertia about the box center of mass, expressed in world coordinates. * denotes the temporary velocity before contact.

Output: V*, ω*. No particle integration or position update occurs here. For zero initial spin, ω* = 0. Gravity is the only applied load in this example.

Example: Δt = 0.005 s and Vⁿ = 0 give V*z = −0.04905 m/s before contact.

2. Evaluate material velocity at the box surface

Start from particle i. Find its nearest point sᵢ on the box surface, then fit Gᵢ from particles within a local neighborhood of i. These surface points come from particles; they are not independently sampled on the box.

ûᵢ = v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹ + Gᵢ (sᵢ − xᵢⁿ)
rᵢ = sᵢ − cⁿ   ·   dᵢ = signed distance − δ

Gᵢ is a regularized local affine fit. dᵢ > 0 means separation; dᵢ < 0 means penetration relative to the offset box surface. δ = 0 in the current selected model.

Output per contact: material velocity ûᵢ, lever arm rᵢ, gap dᵢ, and normal nᵢ pointing from material toward the box. Keep particles already touching or able to reach the box during Δt.

The force routine reads v̂ᵢⁿ⁺¹; it does not produce it. Here it comes from recordings; eventually NeRD must supply it.

3. Iterate contact impulses and box velocity

Start at V*, ω*, jᵢ = 0. Iterate contacts: they can push the box, never pull.

uᵢ = ûᵢ − (jᵢ − α jᵢprev) / mᵢeff   ,   mᵢeff = κ mᵢ
wᵢ = nᵢ · (V + ω × rᵢ − uᵢ) + bᵢ
kᵢ = 1/m + (rᵢ × nᵢ)ᵀ I⁻¹ (rᵢ × nᵢ) + 1/mᵢeff
jᵢn ← max(0, jᵢn − wᵢ / kᵢ)
‖jᵢt‖ ≤ μ jᵢn   ·   friction opposes relative sliding

jᵢ is impulse on the box; n and t denote normal and tangential components. bᵢ = [max(dᵢ, 0) + β min(dᵢ, 0)] / Δt allows approach across a gap and corrects penetration.

For each impulse change Δjᵢ: V ← V + Δjᵢ / m and ω ← ω + I⁻¹(rᵢ × Δjᵢ). Stop when impulse changes are small. Output: V⁺, ω⁺ and jᵢ.

α weights the transported previous impulse (0 = no history). uᵢ is an auxiliary velocity; stored particle velocities stay fixed.

4. Convert the momentum correction into force

Sum the contact impulses on the box. Divide by Δt to obtain the force and torque over this interval.

Σᵢ jᵢ = m (V⁺ − V*)   ⇒   F = Σᵢ jᵢ / Δt
Σᵢ rᵢ × jᵢ = I (ω⁺ − ω*)   ⇒   τ = Σᵢ rᵢ × jᵢ / Δt

Gravity is already included in V*, so F is the material contact force alone. τ is the material contact torque about the box center of mass. I is frozen at the current pose during the solve.

Return: F [N], τ [N m], V⁺ [m/s], ω⁺ [rad/s], and per-particle impulse −jᵢ [N s]. No predicted future box acceleration is required.

Current replay: the rigid-body integrator applies the previously estimated force to match the reference’s one-step coupling delay; it does not directly adopt V⁺ or ω⁺.

Inputs and outputs · select a step to see its equations

The estimator runs no MPM solve. It estimates the dynamic-box reaction; fixed ground and wall reactions are not outputs. HTML tabs show the full derivation.
One force-estimation call takes current particle positions x_i^n, predicted next particle velocities vhat_i^(n+1), particle masses, and current rigid-box position c, orientation R, linear velocity V, angular velocity omega, mass, local inertia, and size. The selected start-geometry model does not consume predicted next particle positions. The motion predictor may need previous particle states, but that is a separate interface and has not yet been implemented. Step 1 keeps geometry fixed and computes V*=V+dt*g and omega*=omega-dt*I^-1[omega cross (I*omega)], using world inertia at the input pose. No particle or box position is advanced in this function. The actual implementation has gravity and gyroscopic motion, not a generic external-load input. Step 2 finds nearest box-surface points, signed distances, and normals. An optional affine fit maps neighboring predicted material velocities to each contact point; it is enabled in the current selected model. In the slide G uses the usual column-vector convention; the NumPy implementation stores the transposed gradient for row vectors. The normal used in the frictional solver points from material toward the box. Candidate contacts are those already touching or able to reach the surface over the timestep using free box velocity and extrapolated material velocity. Step 3 uses projected Gauss-Seidel contact iterations. Impulses start at zero; the previous impulse enters only the effective material response when history is enabled. Its normal part is clamped nonnegative and its tangent is projected into the current friction cone before decay. Effective mass equals particle mass times kappa. Each normal update uses max(0, j_n - w/K_nn), where K = J M^-1 J^T + identity/m_eff in contact coordinates. J maps box generalized velocity to contact-point velocity and M contains box mass and world inertia. The tangential update solves a two-dimensional quadratic within the Coulomb disk and opposes final relative sliding. Every impulse change updates the shared box linear and angular velocity. The production convergence criterion is maximum impulse change below 1e-9 N s; the iteration count has been checked separately. The effective material velocity u is an auxiliary local response; the routine does not write back new particle velocities or positions. This additional response law is a calibrated approximation, not a full granular constitutive equation. Step 4 returns material-on-box force/torque from the generalized momentum correction, corrected body velocities, and equal/opposite particle impulses. Torque is about the box center of mass. In the current diagnostic replay the returned wrench is delayed by one step to match the lagged reference coupler; the returned contact-corrected body velocity is not copied into the trajectory. The replay's body integrator updates position and orientation separately. No future measured body velocity or acceleration is supplied to the estimator. Particle trajectories remain recorded; a fully coupled learned rollout is still untested.

Input: particle positions and velocity history; body poses and velocities; previous contact wrench; material properties and rigid geometry.

The analytical contact solver uses predicted particle velocities and the body's state at t.

The particle model sees the advanced rigid state, computed during deployment rather than copied from a future reference frame. Training supplies recorded advanced rigid geometry. The network decodes a velocity correction and integrates it into particle displacement; next velocity is recovered consistently from that displacement. The analytical contact solver uses current particle positions, predicted next particle velocities, and the pre-transition rigid state, matching the previously evaluated estimator. Its auxiliary particle impulse is not applied again to the network output, which already includes contact motion. This composition does not enforce all particle momentum equations. How the particle model computes one step

Available state → next particle motion

Inputs: current particle positions; three particle-velocity frames; particle mass/radius and material parameters; each rigid body's geometry, pose, velocity, mass, and friction.

The rigid solver advances first using the previous material force. Its computed next body state supplies the GNN's boundary features.

x(t), [v(t−2), v(t−1), v(t)], material, rigid state
↓ GNN + integration
x̂(t+1), v̂(t+1)

Output: every particle's next position and velocity. A separate analytical solver estimates the material-on-body force.

One node per material particle

Rebuild a radius graph each step using Warp GPU neighbor search. Connect both directions for every particle pair within 0.035 m. No region selector is used.

Edge j → i: [ (xⱼ − xᵢ)/r, |xⱼ − xᵢ|/r, (vⱼ − vᵢ)/v₀ ]

7 edge inputs: relative position (3), distance (1), and current relative velocity (3). Here r = 0.035 m and v₀ = 1 m/s.

16 node inputs: velocity history (9), material/particle features (6), and neighbor count divided by 32 (1).

Material features: density/1600, friction, log₁₀(E)/15, Poisson ratio, radius/0.01 m, and mass/0.01 kg.

64 features per node and edge; 3 message-passing layers

A shared boundary encoder reads 9 values per particle/body pair: signed distance, normal (3), relative surface velocity (3), inverse body mass, and friction.

Sum encoded body features into each particle node. Influence decreases linearly to zero at 10 cm separation. There is no shovel/ground/foot category embedding.

eⱼᵢ ← eⱼᵢ + MLP([hⱼ, hᵢ, eⱼᵢ])
aᵢ = Σⱼ eⱼᵢ / √max(1, neighbor count)
hᵢ ← hᵢ + MLP([hᵢ, aᵢ])

Each MLP uses Linear → SiLU → Linear → LayerNorm. All edges update before nodes update. Repeat three times.

The decoder outputs a velocity correction

The final MLP maps 64 node features to three values. Its output is scaled by 0.1 m/s and added to the current velocity plus gravity.

Δvᵢ = (0.1 m/s) × decoder(hᵢ)
v̂ᵢ(t+1) = vᵢ(t) + Δt g + Δvᵢ
x̂ᵢ(t+1) = xᵢ(t) + Δt v̂ᵢ(t+1)

Deployment correction: project particles outside static rigid geometry, then recompute velocity from displacement. The analytical estimator receives that velocity.

Δt = 0.005 s. The decoder has no final LayerNorm. Chunked inference evaluates the same complete graph; it does not omit distant particles.

Predict four particle steps before computing gradients

Start a window from recorded MPM particle state. Feed each predicted particle position and velocity into the next step. During training, rigid-body states at t+1 come from the recording.

Recorded particle state → prediction 1 → 2 → 3 → 4
Loss: weighted squared position error against MPM

At rollout step k, divide position error by Δt × 0.1 m/s × k. Average over coordinates, particles, and four steps. Samples near the moving body receive up to 5× weight.

Velocity noise: 0.003 m/s. Position noise: noise × Δt. AdamW, learning rate 10⁻⁴. No force labels. Training does not backpropagate through the analytical contact solver or a freely evolving rigid body.

Inputs and outputs
Implementation: particle_model.py, graph_inputs(), train_particles.py, and play.py. Selected model: 64 channels, 3 message-passing layers, 3 velocity frames, 4-step training.
The frozen checkpoint is unroll4/best.pt at update 9000. Its decoder outputs a velocity correction, although training targets particle positions. The position correction is dt times that velocity correction. Boundary distance is normalized by 0.1 m and clipped to [-2,2]; boundary influence is max(1-max(normalized distance,0),0). Static bodies have inverse mass zero. The current model does not encode material stress, deformation gradient, or contact-force history. Training starts each four-step window from reference particles and supplies reference advanced rigid states. Deployment uses freely predicted rigid states. Static projection and analytical force reconstruction are outside the training loss. Graph connectivity is discrete; gradients do pass through predicted positions and velocities within the rollout. 24 / ACTUATED G1 CONTACT RECONSTRUCTION GPU IMPLEMENTATION

Actuation enters the robot’s free-motion update

1. Advance the robot

MuJoCo Warp integrates the current robot state, actuator torques, gravity, joint dynamics and previous material feedback.

2. Prepare the foot proxies

Newton copies each foot’s old pose and updated velocity, then removes the previous material impulse using its virtual inertia.

3. Update particles and estimate forces

MPM advances particles using the prepared foot states. The estimator reads current particle positions, next particle velocities, and foot-proxy states, then solves normal contact and friction.

4. Return foot forces

Force = total impulse / Δt. Torque uses contact moment arms about each foot’s center of mass. The next robot step receives these forces.

Scope: this matches Newton’s independent virtual-foot approximation. A fully articulated contact solve would update generalized velocity through M⁻¹Jᵀ and couple both feet.
