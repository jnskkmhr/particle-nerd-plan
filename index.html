XPBD has a defined relationship between its constraint multipliers and forces; ordinary geometric projection does not automatically provide the same result. 15 / MILESTONE 2 METHOD EXPERIMENTAL ASSUMPTION

Estimate body reactions from recorded particle motion

1. Measure acceleration
Use the change in each particle’s velocity.

2. Solve particle force balance
Allow compressive forces between neighbors and friction-limited contact forces.

3. Sum reactions on each body
Apply equal-and-opposite contact forces and their moments about the body center.

Key assumption

A small force penalty selects one solution when motion admits several force distributions. This does not reproduce Newton’s granular material law.

Inputs: MPM particle motion, mass, gravity, and rigid-box geometry. MPM reaction forces are withheld until evaluation.
No neural model is used. All particles participate; ground and moving boxes use the same contact representation.
The implemented inverse problem uses particle position at the end of the interval and acceleration from successive velocities. Internal forces act along neighbor edges and are compressive. Contact forces are nonnegative combinations of four rays inside a Coulomb friction cone. A quadratic penalty of 1e-4 on force coefficients selects a solution. Neighbor radius is 0.065 m and candidate contact distance is 0.04 m. These are experimental assumptions, not a granular constitutive law or a full PBD simulation. The baseline does not evolve material stress history, enforce a sliding friction law, or represent APIC particle angular momentum. Torque is the moment of the estimated contact force about each box center. The cable analogy motivates reconstructing reactions but does not supply the missing particle-material mechanics.

18 / EQUATIONS OF MOTION ONE-DIMENSIONAL EXAMPLE

Equations link the rigid body, particles, and ground

Upward is positive. Each contact force appears with opposite signs on the two objects it connects.

Rigid boxmb ab = U + B − Wb
Particle 1m₁ a₁ = S₁₂ − B − W₁
Particle 2m₂ a₂ = S₂₃ − S₁₂ − W₂
Particle 3m₃ a₃ = G − S₂₃ − W₃
Fixed ground0 = R − G − Wg

m = mass; a = vertical acceleration; W = weight (m g).
U: external actuator force on the box. R: external support force on the ground. Both are signed upward.

Add all five equations:mb ab + m₁ a₁ + m₂ a₂ + m₃ a₃ = U + R − Σi mi g
Contact forces cancel in the sum. The actuator force U and support force R remain.
With known U and measured box acceleration, B follows from the box equation. With prescribed box motion and unknown U, that equation introduces another unknown.
Sum over the box, particles 1–3, and supported ground slab. Vertical translation only; zero ground acceleration does not imply zero contact force.
This schematic extends the following three-particle example to include both rigid objects. The box can move vertically; the ground slab is fixed by an external support. B is the magnitude of the upward force of particle 1 on the box and the downward force of the box on particle 1. S12 is upward on particle 1 and downward on particle 2. S23 is upward on particle 2 and downward on particle 3. G is upward on particle 3 and downward on the ground. U is the signed net external actuator or holding force on the box; R is the signed external force holding the ground slab fixed. Weights are positive magnitudes acting downward. There are no other forces in this example. Summing the five equations cancels B, S12, S23, and G. This removes internal forces from the total balance but also removes the contact forces we want to reconstruct. For specified accelerations and known weights, the six unknowns are B, S12, S23, G, U, R. The five independent balances admit the variation: increase B, S12, S23, G, and R by the same amount, and decrease U by that amount. Every acceleration remains unchanged. This corresponds to the next slide's stationary example with a stronger downward holding force on the box and a stronger upward ground support. If U is independently known and the box acceleration is measured, the box equation instead determines its net contact force in this simple chain. In a forward coupled simulation the next box acceleration is itself an output, so one cannot assume it is independently known to close reconstruction. In three dimensions include vector force balances and rigid-body moment balances, with actuator, joint, and support reactions represented explicitly. This is an illustrative equation chain, not the completed uniqueness analysis for the recorded MPM scene. No MPM solve is introduced at inference.

This example uses force balance and compressive contact only; a material law or a known boundary load could provide the missing information.
This is an exact one-dimensional counterexample, not simulation data. The three particles each weigh 10 N and have zero acceleration. Let B be the downward box force, S12 and S23 be compressive internal forces, and G be the upward ground force. The individual balances are S12-B-10=0, S23-S12-10=0, G-S23-10=0. Thus S12=B+10, S23=B+20, G=B+30. There are three independent scalar equations for four unknown force magnitudes. Any nonnegative B gives a compressive solution. Adding 100 N to all four unknowns changes the box and ground reactions while leaving every particle equation unchanged. The change (100,100,100,100) is a null-space direction of the balance matrix and affects the body reactions. Known contact locations and internal action-reaction are already included. This is stronger than showing ambiguity only in the summed system. It does not prove that every three-dimensional particle scene has ambiguous body reactions; that must be checked for the actual geometry and constraints. It also does not claim that both states satisfy a specified elastic or granular constitutive law with identical history. Such a law is exactly the additional information omitted by force balance alone. The fixed box has an unmeasured holding reaction, so its own equilibrium does not provide a known applied load. Body reactions are opposite to the body-on-particle forces illustrated here.

For the full particle system, write A f = b, where f contains internal-force and contact-force unknowns and b contains known inertial loads after subtracting gravity and other known external loads. Let R f collect per-body forces and moments. If an admissible perturbation d satisfies A d = 0 but R d is nonzero, body reactions are not uniquely determined by the balance equations. A null-space direction that changes only internal forces is harmless for the reaction output. Inequality constraints matter: a linear null-space direction establishes feasible ambiguity only when a nonzero perturbation remains inside compression, friction, and contact conditions. 21 / CURRENT RECONSTRUCTION IMPLEMENTED BASELINE

The current estimate omits most material properties

AssumptionWhat the implementation uses
Particle–particle forcesParticles within 6.5 cm transmit equal-and-opposite compressive forces along their connecting line. No separate tangential force.
Particle–body forcesParticles within 4 cm of a body surface are contact candidates. Forces obey a four-sided approximation of the friction cone.
Choice among possible solutionsA quadratic penalty favors small force coefficients. It supplies no force–deformation relationship.
Current objectiveminimize: force-balance error² + 0.0001 × Σ force coefficient²

Parameters used directly

Particle mass and particle–body friction. Density affects the recorded particle mass.

Material properties not used directly

Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, internal material friction, plasticity, and material history.

With identical motion, masses, geometry, and body friction, changing an omitted material parameter does not change the estimate. It can affect the result indirectly by changing the motion.
Source: current reconstruction implementation. Small force-balance error does not establish accurate forces on individual bodies.
This describes the existing nonlearned force reconstruction, not a trained particle motion model. It solves three translational force-balance equations per material particle. Particle pairs within the neighbor radius carry one nonnegative central-force coefficient. A candidate particle–body contact carries four nonnegative friction-ray coefficients. The positive quadratic regularization penalizes these coefficients; it is not exactly a penalty on the norm of each resultant contact force, because that depends on its ray representation. Appending sqrt(regularization) times the identity with a zero target is equivalent to soft equations favoring zero coefficients. It makes the objective strictly convex in exact arithmetic but does not establish physical correctness. The evaluated baseline solves reached their iteration limit. The code does not read the recorded particle radii, rigid-body velocities or accelerations, Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, internal friction, deformation state, or material history. It computes body torque from contact-force moment arms but does not solve rigid-body dynamics or particle angular-momentum equations. The 4 cm contact shell is a geometric heuristic, not measured grain overlap. The separate DEM-inspired implementation and its measurements are described on the following slides.
22 / DISCRETE ELEMENT METHOD SOURCE OF ADDITIONAL EQUATIONS

Relate each contact force to deformation and motion

A simple noncohesive DEM contact law supplies force values from overlap, relative velocity, and contact history.

Normal force magnitude · active contact

Fn = max(0, kn δ + cn vclose)

Elastic compression + damping; closing speed is positive.
Tangential force vector · before sliding

Ft,trial = −kt ξ − ct vt

ξ accumulates relative tangential displacement during contact.
Sliding limit

‖Ft‖ ≤ μ Fn

Cap the trial force and update history when sliding occurs.
Additional equations: force is related to contact deformation and history, rather than selected only by a small-force preference. Separated particles carry no contact force.
k: contact stiffness; c: damping coefficient; μ: contact friction. Ground and moving bodies use the same law with their own geometry and surface velocity.
Source: LAMMPS granular contact laws and particle–wall contacts. The equations show a linear spring–damper model; Hertz–Mindlin is an alternative.
DEM means discrete element method. The equations illustrate one noncohesive linear spring–dashpot model, not all DEM formulations. For positive overlap, the normal force is the positive part of stiffness times overlap plus damping times signed closing speed. Use zero force after separation; do not apply a damping force across a gap. Tangential velocity is measured at the contact point relative to the other particle or rigid surface, including rotational contributions where represented. Transport tangential history into the changing contact plane, update it over the contact interval, correct it when the Coulomb bound is reached, and reset it when contact is lost. Static friction may remain nonzero at zero instantaneous tangential velocity because of stored tangential displacement. The force on the other object is equal and opposite. Particle–particle contacts can use the same law structure with appropriate radii and contact parameters. Hertz–Mindlin models provide nonlinear normal and tangential relationships involving elastic parameters and contact geometry. These are physical model parameters, so nonlearned does not mean parameter-free. This slide explains the source equations only; it does not claim their direct applicability to Newton MPM material points. References: https://docs.lammps.org/pair_granular.html ; https://docs.lammps.org/fix_wall_gran.html ; https://docs.lammps.org/Howto_granular.html .

28 / ADDING RIGID-BODY DYNAMICS NEW APPROXIMATION

Recover box reactions from contact motion

1. Free box motionAdvance current box velocity under gravity and known loads.
2. Material motionRead the predicted next velocities of particles near the box.
3. Contact solveAdjust box velocity to reduce closing motion at unilateral contacts.
4. Body reactionConvert the box’s momentum correction into contact force and torque.
contact impulse = rigid-body mass matrix × (corrected velocity − free velocity)

Additional physical information

Box mass, inertia, and contact velocity conditions. Measured future box acceleration is not an input.

Explicit approximation

Predicted particle velocities define the material surface motion. An effective contact mass allows compliant relative motion.

Scope: dynamic-box reaction only. Full particle momentum consistency and separate fixed-body reactions remain unresolved.
Reference particle velocities stand in for NeRD predictions in these experiments. No MPM solver or force network runs inside the estimator.
The first variant uses normal contact only; a separate projected-contact iteration adds Coulomb friction.
The previous inverse model treated internal pair forces and body contacts as unknowns constrained by material acceleration. This alternative uses a predicted material velocity field as a contact-motion condition and solves for the dynamic body's response. Inputs are current box pose and velocity, mass and local inertia, particle positions, masses, and predicted next particle velocities. A free velocity includes gravity and gyroscopic acceleration. A six-degree-of-freedom kinetic-energy projection enforces unilateral normal velocity constraints with nonnegative slack. Slack is penalized by an effective contact mass. This finite effective mass is a calibrated approximation, not a claim that an MPM material point is a physical grain. The returned impulse equals the box's momentum correction relative to free motion. The material field is held fixed during reconstruction; compatibility with the full material momentum system remains a separate requirement. The method estimates one dynamic-body wrench, unlike the earlier method that solves all particle equations and all six body reactions. Reference-force labels select scalar approximation parameters on fitting episodes only. Inference receives no force labels and no future box acceleration. The coupler can apply the result in its next rigid-body step. This is not yet a coupled NeRD rollout.
